\subsection{What a probe score is worth}
\label{sec:baselines}

A frozen-backbone probe is usually reported against one control: the same architecture
with random weights. That control establishes that pretraining does something, and
nothing else. In particular it cannot say whether the downstream task was hard enough to
need a representation at all.

We therefore add a second baseline that has no representation and no random weights
either. For each window we compute four moments --- mean, standard deviation, minimum
and maximum --- of every degree of freedom and every channel, giving
$43 \times 5 \times 4 = 860$ numbers per window, and feed them to the same multilabel
linear head the probe uses. The data, the label set, the optimizer and the metrics are
the probe's; the only thing that changes is what enters the head.

The two baselines answer different questions, and only together do they bound the
result. The random encoder says how much of the score is attributable to pretraining.
The moments say how much of the score was available without learning anything.

\paragraph{The readout budget is a confound, and it is not a small one.}
Probe hyperparameters are conventionally chosen once and reported as an implementation
detail. They are not neutral. Table~\ref{tab:budget} evaluates identical frozen
backbones under two readout budgets: the conventional one ($\text{lr}=10^{-3}$, $20$
epochs) and a stronger one ($\text{lr}=5\times10^{-3}$, $40$ epochs).

The random encoder gains $+0.062$ macro mAP from the stronger head --- a $49\%$ relative
improvement obtained without touching the representation. The pretrained encoder gains
$+0.024$ and the moments gain nothing at all. The consequence is that the advantage of
pretraining over the random control, the number this literature normally reports, falls
from $2.75\times$ to $1.97\times$ purely as a function of how the probe was tuned.

This is not a statement about our backbone. It is a statement about the measurement: an
attentive readout over a $T \times D$ token grid can extract substantially more from an
untrained encoder than a weakly-tuned one can, so a probe ratio is only interpretable
alongside the budget that produced it. The moments baseline is invariant to this,
because a fixed $860$-vector into a linear layer has no pooling to learn, which is
precisely what makes it the more stable reference of the two.

\paragraph{Window classification does not separate the hypotheses.}
Under either budget the pretrained encoder beats four summary statistics by between
$7\%$ and $15\%$ macro mAP. A gap that small, on a task where a representation is meant
to be doing the work, indicates that most of what BABEL-60 window classification
rewards is available from the marginal distribution of each channel over the window ---
crudely, how fast and how far each joint moved, without regard to when. We do not read
this as evidence that the representation is poor. We read it as evidence that the task
is weakly discriminative, and we therefore turn to one that is not.

\subsection{Dense prediction separates what from when}
\label{sec:segmentation}

Window classification collapses the time axis before the label is applied. We therefore
evaluate the same frozen backbones on per-patch prediction: each $240$-frame window is
divided into $30$ patches and every patch receives its own multilabel target.

Two properties make this the sharper test. First, a representation that has learned only
window-level statistics cannot express a target that varies within the window. Second,
the moments baseline splits into two arms that bracket the task. The \textsc{patch} arm
computes moments of each patch's own $8$ frames and predicts that patch, so it has
temporal resolution but no context. The \textsc{window} arm computes moments of the
whole window and broadcasts one prediction to all $30$ patches, so it has context and no
temporal resolution whatsoever.

We report two localization metrics alongside the per-patch classification. A boundary is
a patch index where the target changes; $\text{F1}_\partial$ scores boundary detection at
a fixed threshold, and $\text{F1}_\partial^{\text{sweep}}$ takes the best of $30$
thresholds swept on the validation set.

\paragraph{Pretraining helps most where the label varies in time.}
On per-patch macro mAP the pretrained encoder reaches $0.292$ against the random
encoder's $0.108$, a factor of $2.70$. The corresponding factor on window classification
under the same readout budget is $1.97$. On macro F1 the separation is starker still,
$0.228$ against $0.039$.

Crucially this survives the control that eroded the window-classification result. Moving
from the weak to the strong readout, the segmentation ratio falls only from $3.42$ to
$2.70$, while the window-classification ratio falls from $2.75$ to $1.97$; in absolute
terms the pretrained encoder gains $+0.061$ macro mAP from the stronger head against the
random encoder's $+0.041$. Additional readout capacity is worth more on top of a
pretrained representation than on top of an untrained one --- the opposite of what we
observe when the target is a single label for the whole window.

\paragraph{Boundary localization does not benefit.}
The same table shows no such effect on either localization metric: $1.15\times$ on
$\text{F1}_\partial$ and $1.10\times$ on the swept variant. Whatever the pretext task
teaches, it improves the model's account of \emph{which} actions a window contains far
more than its account of \emph{where} they begin and end.

The moments make the point sharper. Their \textsc{patch} arm reaches $0.132$
$\text{F1}_\partial$ against the pretrained encoder's $0.140$ --- a gap of $6\%$, where the
same comparison on per-patch macro mAP is $0.195$ against $0.292$, a gap of $50\%$. Eight
frames of summary statistics, with no context and no learned representation, localize
action boundaries almost as well as a pretrained transformer does. Boundary detection on
this data is very nearly solved by local signal energy.

The swept metric deserves particular scepticism. A random encoder reaches $0.188$ under
it, and randomly generated per-patch scores with no temporal structure at all reach
0.148 when swept identically. Nearly the entire observed spread is threshold
fitting rather than localization. We report $\text{F1}_\partial$ at a fixed threshold as
the honest column and retain the swept variant only to make this point.

That the \textsc{window} moments arm scores exactly $0.000$ on both boundary metrics is
worth stating plainly: it confirms the metrics measure what they claim to, since a
predictor with no temporal resolution is assigned no localization credit whatsoever.

\paragraph{On the annotations.}
BABEL supplies both frame-level and sequence-level action labels. Sequence-level spans
cover $0$ to the full duration exactly, so they are constant across every window they
touch and can never produce a boundary. Retaining them left $42\%$ of segmentation
windows with a target constant in time and $40\%$ of samples contributing gradient to
\emph{what} and none to \emph{when}. All segmentation results use frame-level
annotations only, with sequence-only samples dropped rather than retained as
all-negative windows; this raises the boundary rate from $0.052$ to $0.080$ at the cost
of $26\%$ of the windows.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Each arm here is a single seed. Under the weak readout, where we have two seeds of the
pretrained backbone, the seed spread on window classification is $0.001$ macro mAP with
attentive pooling, which we take as the noise floor; we have not established the
corresponding floor for the strong readout or for segmentation. The random encoder had
not converged at the end of training on either segmentation arm, so the ratios reported
against it are upper bounds. Finally, the pretrained backbone reaches its best
$\text{F1}_\partial^{\text{sweep}}$ at epoch $4$ and its best macro mAP at epoch $39$:
the two objectives trade off against each other during probe training, and a single
checkpoint cannot be optimal for both.
